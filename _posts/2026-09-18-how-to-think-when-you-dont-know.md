*This essay began with a simple question: could the useful ideas in Annie Duke’s* Thinking in Bets *be expressed more crisply than they are in a full book? The answer led somewhere broader. The framework below draws on Duke’s distinction between decision quality and outcome quality; Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky’s work on judgment under uncertainty; Philip Tetlock’s research on probabilistic forecasting and calibration; Gary Klein’s premortem; Ray Dalio’s idea of believability-weighted judgment; Amazon’s language of one-way and two-way doors and “disagree and commit”; and the strategy literature on real options. The goal is not to summarize those thinkers one by one. It is to combine the strongest parts into a practical system for making consequential business decisions when the answer is not yet knowable.*

Most important business decisions have to be made before the facts are available. You hire someone before you know whether they will perform. You enter a market before you know how competitors will react. You fund a product before you know whether customers will care. You reorganize a team before you know whether the new structure will work. You make a large technical investment before you know how quickly the underlying technology will change.

Then reality arrives, and the mind begins rewriting history.

A successful product makes the strategy look obvious. A failed launch makes the original decision look foolish. An employee who becomes a star seems like proof of a brilliant hiring process. A risky shortcut that works makes the risk seem smaller than it was. Once the outcome is visible, it becomes difficult to reconstruct what was actually knowable beforehand.

That is the central problem of judgment under uncertainty. **Decisions are made with partial information; outcomes are observed with much more information.** If we judge the former entirely through the latter, experience teaches us the wrong lessons.

A good decision process therefore has to do two things. Before the decision, it has to represent uncertainty honestly enough to make a sensible bet. After the decision, it has to learn from the outcome without pretending that the outcome was inevitable.

This essay develops one practical loop for doing that:

1. **Start outside the story.** Before becoming persuaded by the specifics of your case, ask what usually happens in comparable cases.
2. **Make the bet explicit.** State what you believe will happen, with what probability, and identify the assumptions carrying the decision.
3. **Reduce the uncertainty that matters.** Find the few load-bearing assumptions, then buy information about them rather than analyzing everything.
4. **Shape the commitment around uncertainty.** Move quickly on reversible decisions; stage expensive or irreversible commitments so that learning can occur before the full bet is made.
5. **Use disagreement intelligently.** Seek dissent, weight relevant track record, clarify who decides, then commit.
6. **Learn without rewriting history.** Record what you believed before the outcome, compare prediction with reality, and improve the process across many decisions.

If you read only this far, that is the framework. The rest of the essay explains why each part matters, how the pieces fit together, and how to apply them in a real organization.

## Why good judgment is harder than it looks

The difficulty begins with the way humans naturally think. We prefer stories to distributions, confidence to ambiguity, and explanations to probabilities. We are very good at constructing coherent accounts of why something happened. We are much worse at remembering how uncertain it looked before it happened.

Kahneman and Tversky’s classic work on judgment under uncertainty documented several of the shortcuts behind this. People judge likelihood by resemblance, by what comes easily to mind, and by anchors that may have little connection to reality. These heuristics are often useful, but they also generate systematic errors. In business, one of the most important is base-rate neglect: the tendency to focus on the vivid specifics of our situation while underweighting what usually happens in situations of the same general kind.

A team considering a new enterprise product may spend weeks discussing its exceptional technology, the quality of the founding team, customer enthusiasm, and strategic fit. All of that may matter. But the team may never ask the colder question: *How often do products at this stage, with this adoption pattern and this distribution model, actually reach the scale we are forecasting?*

The inside story is psychologically compelling because it contains all the information close at hand. The outside view feels crude because it deliberately ignores much of that detail at first. Yet the outside view is often where disciplined judgment should begin.

There is a second problem. Ordinary business language hides uncertainty. “I think this will work.” “The launch is likely to be successful.” “We should be able to ship in Q3.” “The integration risk is manageable.” These statements sound precise enough to support action, but they can conceal radically different beliefs.

One executive may mean a 55% chance of success when she says “likely.” Another may mean 85%. They appear aligned until somebody asks for a number.

This is why Tetlock’s forecasting work is so useful beyond forecasting itself. It encourages people to express uncertain beliefs as probabilities, update them as evidence arrives, and keep score. The purpose is not numerical theater. A number such as 65% is not magically scientific. Its value is that it exposes the strength of a belief and makes disagreement inspectable.

A third problem appears after the decision. Annie Duke uses the term *resulting* for judging the quality of a decision primarily by its outcome. It is one of the easiest mistakes to recognize in other people and one of the hardest to avoid ourselves.

Imagine Anna puts all her savings into a random cryptocurrency because someone at a party tells her it will double. It does. Ben builds a diversified portfolio after careful analysis, and the market crashes the next month. Anna got the better outcome. Ben made the better decision.

The exaggerated example is obvious. Real business decisions are harder because the quality of the reasoning and the outcome are usually correlated. Better decisions do tend to produce better results over time. The mistake is treating one realized outcome as if it perfectly revealed the quality of the process that produced it.

Good judgment therefore requires a method that works on both sides of the decision: a way to make uncertainty visible beforehand and a way to preserve the original state of knowledge afterward.

## The framework in one page

Before going deeper, it helps to see the whole process as a single loop.

**Begin with a reference class.** Ask what normally happens to decisions like this one. This gives you a prior probability before the details of your situation begin pulling you toward a compelling story.

**Then incorporate the specifics.** What is genuinely different about this case? Which evidence should move you away from the base rate? How much should it move you? This is where domain expertise belongs: after the outside view has established an anchor, not before.

**Decompose the decision.** Replace “this product will succeed” with the smaller propositions underneath it. Customers have the problem. The solution is technically feasible. The workflow is good enough to earn trust. Distribution can reach the right users. Economics work at scale. The organization can execute. Somewhere in that chain are the assumptions carrying most of the uncertainty.

**Buy information about those assumptions.** Run the pilot, prototype the hard technical component, test willingness to pay, interview the skeptical customers, simulate the migration, or stage the rollout. The purpose of experimentation is not simply to “validate the idea.” It is to change the probability distribution enough to support a better next decision.

**Match commitment to uncertainty and reversibility.** If you can reverse the decision cheaply, make it faster. If it is expensive, irreversible, or capable of threatening the business, demand more evidence or find a way to stage it. When uncertainty is high, the most valuable asset is often the option to wait, learn, and decide again.

**Stress-test with other minds.** Ask how the plan could fail. Seek out people who disagree for substantive reasons. Weight their opinions by relevant experience and quality of reasoning rather than title or confidence. Then make decision rights explicit so debate does not become paralysis.

**Write down what you expect.** Record the probability, assumptions, major risks, and what evidence would change your mind. Later, compare that record with what happened. The outcome is now evidence for updating the model rather than an invitation to rewrite history.

The rest of the article expands those steps.

## 1. Start outside the story

A disciplined decision begins with a question that feels almost deliberately unsophisticated: **what usually happens?**

Philip Tetlock often describes strong forecasters as starting with an “outside view.” Before getting absorbed in the special facts of the current case, they establish a base rate using comparable cases. Only then do they move to the “inside view” and adjust for what is genuinely distinctive.

Suppose a company is evaluating whether a new internal AI platform will reach 40% adoption within a year. The inside view is full of details: enthusiastic pilot teams, a strong model, executive sponsorship, a clever distribution plan, perhaps a handful of striking success stories. The outside view asks different questions. How quickly do internal developer tools normally diffuse? What fraction of voluntary tools reach 40% adoption in a year? What happened to previous tools with similar switching costs? How often does strong pilot enthusiasm translate into broad habitual use?

The base rate will rarely answer the question. It gives you a starting point.

That distinction matters because every case really is different. Blindly applying a historical average can be as foolish as ignoring it. The skill lies in moving from the outside view to the inside view deliberately.

Imagine comparable internal tools have historically had a 25% chance of reaching the target within a year. You now have a prior. The next question is not “why is our product great?” It is “what evidence in this case is strong enough to move us away from 25%, and by how much?”

Perhaps usage in the pilot is three times higher than historical pilots. Perhaps installation is automatic rather than voluntary. Perhaps the tool replaces an already mandatory workflow. Those facts could justify moving the probability substantially. A charismatic demo or an executive saying the product feels inevitable should move it much less.

This structure protects against one of the most common forms of organizational optimism: treating every strategic initiative as an exception before it has earned the right to be one.

### Turn vague beliefs into probabilities

Once you have a rough prior, express the decision in probabilistic terms. “This will work” is not useful enough. “I currently estimate a 60% chance that we reach 40% weekly active adoption within twelve months” is much better.

Again, the number is not the point. The number is a forcing function.

It prompts useful questions. Why 60 rather than 40? Which pieces of evidence are doing the most work? What would make the probability 75? What observation would push it below 30? Two people who disagree can compare assumptions instead of trading rhetoric.

Probabilities also let beliefs move without turning every update into a political reversal. A leader who moves from 70% to 45% after new evidence is not necessarily “flip-flopping.” She may be doing exactly what rational judgment requires.

The relevant question is not whether the belief changed. It is whether it changed appropriately in response to evidence.

Tetlock’s work also introduces the idea of calibration. If you make many predictions at 70% confidence, roughly 70% should eventually be correct. If 95% of them are correct, you are underconfident. If 45% are correct, you are overconfident. Calibration is useful because it turns judgment from a personality trait into something that can be measured and improved.

A single forecast cannot tell you whether you are calibrated. A sequence can.

This is an important organizational shift. Companies often evaluate people on the persuasiveness of individual decisions rather than the accuracy of their judgment over time. The charismatic leader who speaks in certainties can look stronger than the calibrated leader who says “65%.” A system that keeps score can eventually reverse that incentive.

### Decompose the probability

A single probability is useful, but the next step is more important: decompose it.

“This product has a 60% chance of succeeding” still hides the mechanism. What has to be true?

For a product decision, the assumptions might look something like this:

- the problem is common and important enough to change behavior;
- the product solves enough of the problem to create repeated use;
- reliability is high enough to earn trust;
- distribution reaches users at the relevant moment;
- switching costs are acceptable;
- the business or organizational economics work;
- the team can execute within the required window.

Now ask which assumptions are both important and uncertain.

Perhaps technical feasibility is almost certain but habitual adoption is unclear. Perhaps demand is obvious but distribution is weak. Perhaps the product works in isolation but only if another team delivers a dependency.

Those are the load-bearing assumptions. The entire probability may depend disproportionately on one or two of them.

This decomposition is one of the bridges between forecasting and strategy. Once a belief becomes a set of assumptions, the team can stop arguing abstractly about whether the plan is “good” and start asking what it needs to learn.

## 2. Reduce the uncertainty that matters

Organizations often say they need “more data.” That phrase is too vague to be useful.

More information has value only if it can materially change the decision.

This is the logic behind the value of information. Suppose you are deciding whether to spend $20 million entering a market. If a $100,000 experiment can resolve the assumption carrying most of the uncertainty, that experiment may be extraordinarily valuable. If another month of research is unlikely to change the decision at all, the research may be mostly delay.

A useful question is: **what would we most like to know before making the full commitment, and what is the cheapest credible way to learn it?**

This sounds simple, but it changes how teams experiment. Many product experiments are designed to generate evidence that the product is promising. A better experiment is designed to discriminate between competing models of reality.

If the key uncertainty is willingness to pay, free engagement is weak evidence. If the key uncertainty is whether a migration can be performed safely at scale, a polished demo is almost irrelevant. If the key uncertainty is whether users will incorporate a tool into a weekly workflow, satisfaction after a single trial may tell you little.

The experiment should attack the load-bearing assumption directly.

### Distinguish uncertainty from ignorance

Not all uncertainty can be reduced.

Sometimes the world is genuinely stochastic. You can know everything reasonably knowable about a coin toss and still not know what the next toss will be. Sometimes the missing information exists and can be gathered. Sometimes the problem is not data but model uncertainty: you have observations but do not understand the causal system well enough to predict it.

These cases call for different responses.

If information is available cheaply, gather it. If the mechanism is unclear, design an experiment that reveals structure. If uncertainty is irreducible, stop pretending that another spreadsheet will make it disappear.

This is especially important in large organizations, where analysis can become a socially acceptable way to postpone commitment. Another forecast, another review, another deck, another market study: the organization looks busy while the core uncertainty remains untouched.

A decision process should therefore contain a stopping rule. What information could plausibly change the decision? How expensive is that information? How long will it take? If the likely value of learning is lower than the cost of delay, decide.

### Use a premortem before the commitment

One of the best ways to identify hidden uncertainty is Gary Klein’s premortem. The idea is simple: instead of asking whether the plan will succeed, assume that it is a year later and the plan has failed badly. Then ask everyone involved to write down plausible reasons why.

This works partly because it changes the social situation. In an ordinary planning meeting, raising a failure mode can sound like opposition. In a premortem, finding failure modes is the assignment. People who have reservations no longer have to position themselves as blockers.

The technique also counteracts the momentum that develops around a preferred plan. Once a project has a name, sponsor, team, deck, and launch date, discussion naturally shifts toward execution. Premortems reopen the question of what the team may be missing.

The output should not become an enormous risk register. The point is to find failure modes that are both plausible and consequential, then connect them to evidence.

If the premortem says the product could fail because onboarding is too difficult, how will you know? If the concern is that a critical partner team will not prioritize the dependency, what signal would reveal that early? If the risk is that customers like the demo but never form a habit, what behavior should you measure?

This converts a generic fear into a testable condition.

## 3. Shape the commitment around uncertainty

Once you know what you believe and what remains uncertain, the next question is how much to commit.

This is where many decision frameworks become too abstract. They focus on choosing the best option but say less about the size, timing, and reversibility of the bet.

In business, those are often more important than the binary choice.

Amazon popularized a useful metaphor: some decisions are one-way doors and some are two-way doors. A two-way-door decision can be reversed without catastrophic cost. A one-way-door decision is difficult or expensive to undo. The point is not merely descriptive. The two kinds of decisions should use different processes.

Reversible decisions can be made with less information and corrected quickly. Bezos argued that many decisions should be made with roughly 70% of the information one wishes one had, because waiting for near-certainty can make an organization too slow.

The principle is broader than Amazon: **the cost of being wrong should determine how much certainty you demand before acting.**

If a product setting can be changed tomorrow, a long approval chain is probably wasteful. If a migration will lock thousands of customers into a new architecture, more scrutiny makes sense. If the organization treats every decision as irreversible, speed collapses. If it treats irreversible commitments casually, occasional mistakes become existential.

### Real options: pay for the right to decide later

The strategy literature on real options provides a deeper version of the same idea.

A financial option gives you the right, without the obligation, to make a larger transaction later. A real option applies that logic to business decisions. You make a smaller investment now to preserve the ability to make a larger investment after uncertainty resolves.

A pilot is often a real option. So is a prototype, an exploratory partnership, a limited market launch, a short-term contract, or an architecture that preserves the ability to switch suppliers.

The important insight is that uncertainty can increase the value of flexibility.

Traditional planning often treats uncertainty as something to eliminate before acting. Real-options thinking asks how to structure the decision so that learning itself has value. The organization makes an initial commitment large enough to gain information and preserve opportunity, but small enough to avoid locking into the full downside.

This creates a very practical rule: **when uncertainty is high, do not merely lower confidence; redesign the commitment.**

Suppose a company is unsure whether a new AI system can support a mission-critical workflow. The choice does not have to be “adopt” or “do not adopt.” The company can build the integration behind an abstraction layer, deploy it to a subset of traffic, maintain a fallback, and define clear expansion criteria. Each step creates information that improves the next decision.

This is one reason staged investments are so powerful. They convert a large forecast into a sequence of smaller forecasts, each informed by more evidence than the last.

### Expected value, downside, and survival

Probabilities also need to be combined with consequences.

A 90% chance of gaining $1 and a 10% chance of losing $1 million is a terrible bet. A 20% chance of a large gain may be attractive if the downside is small and repeatable. Expected value provides a simple language for combining probability and payoff.

But expected value is not sufficient when the downside can remove you from the game.

Imagine a strategy with excellent average returns and a small probability of complete ruin. If repeated long enough, that ruin probability can dominate everything else. A company that loses its ability to operate cannot benefit from the attractive average return that might have existed across hypothetical worlds.

This is why survival constraints matter. Cash reserves, position limits, redundancy, staged rollouts, safety margins, and limits on leverage all serve a similar purpose: they preserve the ability to continue making bets.

The right question is therefore not simply “Which option has the highest expected value?” It is also “What happens if the bad tail occurs, and can we survive it?”

This distinction is particularly important for leaders who become comfortable with risk because previous risks worked. A string of successful outcomes may say more about luck than about the safety of the underlying process.

## 4. Use other minds without creating paralysis

Individual judgment has limits. Other people can see evidence you missed, carry different models of the world, or have experience with failure modes you have never encountered.

But “get diverse opinions” is not enough. Organizations need a method for deciding whose judgment should matter, how disagreement should be handled, and when debate ends.

### Treat disagreement as information

Suppose you estimate a 75% chance that an acquisition will succeed. A colleague you respect says 35%.

The least useful response is to begin defending your conclusion. The useful question is: **what does this person know, believe, or weight differently from me?**

Perhaps you agree that the technology is complementary but disagree about organizational integration. Perhaps one of you has direct experience with similar acquisitions. Perhaps you are using different base rates. Perhaps you agree on the facts but have different assumptions about how quickly the market will change.

Good disagreement decomposes the decision.

Instead of arguing about “good acquisition versus bad acquisition,” you may discover that the real disagreement is over a narrower claim: whether the acquired engineering team will remain intact for two years. That question may be much easier to investigate.

### Weight judgment by relevant believability

Ray Dalio’s idea of believability-weighted decision making adds another useful layer. Opinions should not be treated as equally informative simply because everyone has one. Dalio argues that greater weight should go to people who have repeatedly succeeded in the relevant domain and can explain the cause-and-effect reasoning behind their view.

That is directionally right, but it needs an important qualification: **believability is domain-specific.**

A brilliant infrastructure engineer may have an excellent track record on scalability and a mediocre track record predicting customer behavior. A senior executive may understand organizational politics better than a product team and know less about the day-to-day user problem. Someone who succeeded in one market regime may be carrying a model that does not transfer to another.

Believability should therefore update your judgment, not substitute for it. Relevant track record matters. Quality of reasoning matters. Direct access to current evidence matters. Title by itself should matter very little.

There is also a connection back to calibration. If an organization keeps enough records, believability does not have to be based purely on reputation. Some forms of judgment can be scored over time. Who is consistently well calibrated about delivery dates? Who estimates technical feasibility accurately? Who is good at predicting adoption? Who repeatedly misses organizational dependencies?

A track record makes expertise more legible.

### Use premortems and dissent before commitment

Dissent is most valuable before the decision hardens.

This is where Klein’s premortem and Dalio’s thoughtful disagreement complement each other. The premortem creates permission to surface failure modes. Believability helps determine which warnings deserve particular weight. The decision owner can then incorporate the strongest objections into the model.

One useful practice is to ask the most credible skeptic to explain the strongest case against the preferred option. Another is to ask the decision owner to state what evidence from the dissenters actually changed the plan.

The purpose is not to manufacture consensus. It is to improve the decision.

### Separate the right to challenge from the right to decide

Organizations often confuse open debate with shared decision rights.

They are different.

A healthy decision system can give many people the right—and often the responsibility—to challenge an important decision while still making it explicit who ultimately decides.

Dalio makes this distinction in his own framework, warning against confusing the right to advise and debate with the right to make the decision. Amazon’s “disagree and commit” solves the same organizational problem from another direction. Once the relevant arguments have been heard and the decision owner has decided, continuing to relitigate the decision can become more expensive than the remaining uncertainty.

This does not mean suppressing new evidence. If reality changes materially, reopen the decision. It means that unresolved disagreement cannot become a permanent operating state.

A good decision process therefore has two phases with different norms:

**Before the decision:** invite dissent, expose assumptions, reward people for identifying uncomfortable evidence.

**After the decision:** align execution, monitor the agreed signals, and reopen only when evidence crosses a meaningful threshold.

This combination is harder than either consensus or autocracy. It asks leaders to tolerate serious challenge before commitment and asks dissenters to support execution afterward.

But it is one of the clearest ways to get both decision quality and organizational speed.

## 5. Learn from outcomes without rewriting history

The final step is also the beginning of the next decision.

An outcome should update your model. The difficulty is extracting the right lesson.

Imagine a team estimates a 70% chance that a product launch will reach its adoption target. The launch succeeds. What did the team learn?

Less than it may feel like.

A 70% event happening is unsurprising. The success does not prove the team’s assumptions were correct. Perhaps adoption came from a distribution channel nobody anticipated. Perhaps a major risk was real but happened not to materialize. Perhaps the target was reached even though the underlying retention model was wrong.

Likewise, if the launch fails, the original 70% estimate was not automatically foolish. Thirty-percent events happen.

This is the heart of Duke’s distinction between decision quality and outcome quality. Over enough repetitions, better processes should produce better outcomes. But one outcome is noisy evidence.

### Keep a decision record

The best defense against hindsight is to preserve the original state of knowledge.

For an important decision, write down:

- the decision and alternatives;
- the relevant base rate or reference class;
- the probability assigned to the important outcomes;
- the assumptions carrying the forecast;
- the major downside risks;
- the evidence that would cause a change of course;
- the signals you expect to observe over the next 30, 90, or 180 days.

This can fit on one page. The goal is not documentation for its own sake. It is creating a time capsule.

Months later, you can compare the original model with reality. Which assumptions were correct? Which were wrong? Which risks mattered? Which supposedly important uncertainties turned out to be irrelevant? Which events were never considered at all?

Without that record, memory will quietly alter the original forecast to make the outcome look more predictable than it was.

### Define kill criteria before attachment grows

One of the hardest business decisions is stopping something that once looked promising.

The problem is not merely sunk cost in the economic sense. Projects accumulate identity, teams, sponsors, reputations, roadmaps, and stories about why the next milestone will change everything. As attachment grows, evidence that would once have changed the decision gets reinterpreted as a temporary setback.

A useful defense is to define exit or change criteria before the evidence arrives.

If adoption remains below X after two quarters, what happens? If the technical constraint is not solved by a certain milestone, do we stop or change architecture? If customer acquisition cost exceeds a threshold, what is the response? If the strategic partner misses two commitments, do we redesign the plan?

These are not automatic rules. New information may legitimately change them. Their value is that they force the team to confront in advance what evidence would count against the thesis.

That makes later discussion less vulnerable to motivated reasoning.

### Evaluate a portfolio of decisions

The quality of judgment becomes visible over sequences.

One product bet may fail. One hiring decision may go badly. One acquisition may outperform every expectation. None of those outcomes, by themselves, tells you whether the underlying decision system is good.

Look instead across categories of decisions.

Are estimates of delivery dates calibrated? Do 70% product forecasts succeed roughly 70% of the time? Which assumptions repeatedly surprise the organization? Are teams systematically overconfident about adoption and underconfident about technical execution? Does one group identify dependency risk better than others? Do premortems surface risks that later matter?

This turns postmortems from storytelling exercises into feedback systems.

The deeper goal is not to eliminate mistakes. An organization taking meaningful bets will be wrong regularly. The goal is to distinguish productive error from careless error, update faster, and become better calibrated about where the organization does and does not have genuine predictive skill.

## A practical decision protocol

The framework can be reduced to a protocol for consequential decisions. This should not be applied mechanically to everything. Most day-to-day choices deserve far less process. Use it when the stakes, uncertainty, or irreversibility justify the effort.

### 1. Frame the decision

State the decision in a form that can actually be resolved.

Bad: “What should our AI strategy be?”

Better: “Should we invest 25 engineers for the next twelve months in building an internal AI coding platform, or continue relying primarily on external tools?”

Identify the meaningful alternatives, including doing nothing.

### 2. Establish the outside view

Choose a reference class.

What happened to comparable internal platforms? How quickly did they reach adoption? What did similar build-versus-buy decisions cost? How often did switching costs prevent migration?

Write down a rough base rate before discussing why this case is special.

### 3. State the current belief

Express the important outcomes probabilistically.

“We estimate a 60% chance of reaching 40% weekly active use within twelve months, a 25% chance of reaching 15–40%, and a 15% chance of failing to establish meaningful habitual adoption.”

Precision should match the quality of evidence. The point is to expose uncertainty, not manufacture certainty.

### 4. Decompose the bet

Ask what has to be true.

List the critical assumptions, then identify the two or three that are both uncertain and capable of changing the decision.

Do not create a fifty-item risk register. Find the load-bearing beliefs.

### 5. Buy the most valuable information

Ask which experiment, pilot, customer test, technical prototype, or external evidence could change your probability most.

Prefer tests that distinguish between hypotheses rather than tests designed to produce encouraging results.

Set a stopping rule for analysis.

### 6. Run a premortem

Assume the decision failed badly. Ask the relevant participants to explain why.

Compare the answers. Which failure modes appear repeatedly? Which come from people with relevant track records? Which can be monitored or tested before full commitment?

Update the decision model.

### 7. Shape the commitment

Classify the decision by reversibility and downside.

Can the initial commitment be smaller? Can the architecture preserve optionality? Can the rollout be staged? Can you maintain a fallback? Can you define expansion gates?

Protect against outcomes that threaten survival or create disproportionate irreversible cost.

### 8. Resolve disagreement

Identify the people whose judgment is especially relevant and why.

Surface the strongest case against the preferred option. Make sure the decision owner can explain what the dissenters believe rather than merely rebutting them.

Then make the decision owner explicit.

### 9. Define update and exit criteria

What evidence would cause you to accelerate, reduce investment, change direction, or stop?

Record the thresholds before organizational attachment grows.

### 10. Record the forecast and decide

Write down the decision, probabilities, assumptions, risks, and expected signals.

Then act.

A decision process that never ends in a decision is just analysis.

## Worked example: should we build the platform?

Consider a large software company deciding whether to build an internal AI coding platform. The proposal requires 25 engineers for a year. Advocates believe owning the platform will create strategic leverage, improve integration with internal systems, and reduce dependence on external vendors. Skeptics argue that foundation-model and coding-tool capabilities are improving so quickly that an internal platform will struggle to keep pace.

The naive process would begin with competing narratives. One side presents a vision of deep integration, proprietary context, and strategic control. The other presents a story about commodity models, fast-moving vendors, and wasted engineering effort. Both stories can be persuasive.

The framework starts somewhere else.

### Start with the reference class

The team looks at previous internal developer platforms requiring developers to change habitual workflows. It finds that broad voluntary adoption is difficult: only a minority reach 40% weekly use within a year unless they replace an existing mandatory surface or deliver a large, obvious productivity gain.

Suppose the rough base rate for reaching the target is 25%.

That number is not destiny. It is the starting point.

The team then asks what is different here. AI coding has unusually strong organic demand. Developers are already using external alternatives. The company can integrate proprietary code and tooling unavailable to vendors. On the other hand, external products are improving quickly, and internal teams historically underestimate the cost of building polished developer experiences.

After discussing the evidence, the decision group moves the probability from 25% to 55%.

Already the conversation is better. Instead of “we believe strongly in this platform,” the team has a prior, explicit adjustments, and a probability that can move.

### Decompose the bet

The team identifies four load-bearing assumptions:

1. proprietary internal context creates a meaningful advantage over external tools;
2. the platform can deliver reliability close enough to leading external products;
3. developers will tolerate switching costs in exchange for integration benefits;
4. the internal team can keep pace with a rapidly changing technical ecosystem.

The group is highly confident about access to proprietary context. It is moderately confident about reliability. The two largest uncertainties are whether proprietary context actually changes developer behavior and whether the team can remain competitive as the external market moves.

These become the targets for learning.

### Buy information before buying the whole strategy

Instead of immediately staffing 25 engineers for a year, the company funds an eight-person team for twelve weeks.

The goal is not to build a small version of the entire platform. The goal is to test the two load-bearing assumptions.

The team integrates proprietary code context into an existing coding surface and runs an experiment with developers who already use external tools. It measures whether internal context meaningfully improves task completion and whether those improvements are large enough to change habitual use.

In parallel, the team estimates the engineering cost of keeping model, agent, evaluation, and IDE integrations current across several external model generations.

This is a real option. The company spends a smaller amount to preserve the opportunity to make the larger investment after learning more.

### Run the premortem

Before the pilot starts, the team assumes that a year has passed and the platform has failed.

Engineers say the likely cause is maintenance burden: every model or IDE change breaks integrations. Product leaders say developers may appreciate internal context but still prefer the UX of external tools. Security specialists worry that controls will make the internal product materially slower. Finance points out that the economic case assumes very high adoption.

One concern appears repeatedly: the strategy assumes the company must own the entire end-to-end coding surface to capture the value of proprietary context.

That assumption was not explicit in the original proposal.

The team adds a competing hypothesis: perhaps the durable asset should be the context and evaluation layer, while the user-facing coding surface remains interchangeable.

The premortem has changed the option set, not merely added risks to a spreadsheet.

### Use believability carefully

The CTO believes deeply in owning the platform. Several senior engineers disagree.

Rather than treating this as hierarchy versus consensus, the decision owner asks whose judgment is most relevant to each uncertainty.

The CTO has strong experience with strategic platform investments. The senior engineers have better recent experience maintaining integrations with rapidly changing AI APIs. Developer productivity researchers have the best evidence about workflow adoption. Procurement understands vendor economics.

No single person has maximum believability across the whole problem.

The team weights the arguments accordingly.

This is a more useful interpretation of believability than simply trusting the most successful or senior person in the room.

### Define the decision and the exit criteria

Before beginning the pilot, the company writes down its current forecast:

- 55% chance that a full internal platform could reach 40% weekly developer adoption within twelve months;
- 70% chance that proprietary context creates measurable task-level improvement;
- 40% chance that the benefit is large enough to make developers switch from leading external tools;
- significant uncertainty about the annual maintenance burden.

It also defines what would change the plan.

If proprietary context does not materially improve real coding tasks, stop the platform strategy.

If context helps but developers still strongly prefer external surfaces, invest in a context/evaluation layer that can plug into multiple tools.

If both context and the integrated UX produce large sustained gains, increase investment.

Now the pilot has a purpose beyond “show progress.” Its job is to decide which larger bet deserves to be made.

### Learn from the result

Suppose the pilot shows that proprietary context improves task success substantially, but developers still prefer the external coding surface because of speed and UX.

A team attached to the original strategy might call the pilot a mixed result and continue building the full platform.

A decision-oriented team asks what the evidence did to the model.

The first assumption strengthened. The third weakened. The evidence supports owning differentiated context more strongly than owning the entire surface.

The company changes strategy.

That does not mean the original proposal was foolish. The proposal created the experiment that revealed the better architecture. The decision process worked because the commitment was staged and the team had defined in advance what evidence should change its mind.

This is what decision-making under uncertainty looks like when it becomes an operating system rather than a philosophical idea.

## What the framework is really trying to accomplish

All of these techniques—base rates, probabilities, premortems, real options, believability, decision journals—can look like separate management tools. They are more useful when seen as parts of one system.

The system is trying to prevent four predictable failures.

The first is **story capture**: falling in love with the specific narrative before asking what usually happens.

The second is **false precision or false certainty**: hiding uncertainty inside vague language and then making commitments as though the future were known.

The third is **commitment ahead of learning**: making the full bet before resolving the uncertainties that could have been tested cheaply.

The fourth is **hindsight rewriting**: allowing the outcome to overwrite the beliefs, probabilities, and risks that actually existed when the decision was made.

A good decision system creates friction against all four.

It starts with the outside view before allowing the inside story to dominate. It makes beliefs explicit enough to challenge and update. It buys information where learning is valuable. It preserves flexibility when uncertainty is high. It creates room for dissent while keeping decision rights clear. And it records the original model so that outcomes can improve future judgment.

None of this guarantees good outcomes.

That is precisely the point.

In an uncertain world, a method that guarantees good outcomes does not exist. The ambition is more modest and more useful: make better bets, learn faster, survive errors, and become progressively better calibrated about the parts of the world you actually understand.

## Further reading and intellectual lineage

The framework above is a synthesis. These are the sources that most directly shaped it:

- **Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets*** — the clearest popular treatment of separating decision quality from outcome quality, “resulting,” and probabilistic thinking. See Duke’s [discussion of the framework](https://www.annieduke.com/annie-duke-thinking-in-bets/).
- **Annie Duke, *Quit*** — a useful extension of the framework into stopping decisions, escalation of commitment, and defining conditions for quitting.
- **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, [“Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases”](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124)** — the foundational account of representativeness, availability, anchoring, and systematic errors in probabilistic judgment.
- **Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner, *Superforecasting*** — probabilities, calibration, updating, base rates, the outside view, and the idea that forecasting skill can be trained and measured. Tetlock gives a concise explanation of the approach in this [Wharton interview](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/why-an-open-mind-is-key-to-making-better-predictions/).
- **Gary Klein, [“Performing a Project Premortem”](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)** — a compact technique for surfacing risks and dissent before commitment.
- **Ray Dalio, *Principles*** — [believability-weighted judgment](https://www.principles.com/principles/633d5d13-8610-425f-ad62-cd62347d9165) and the distinction between thoughtful disagreement and decision rights.
- **Jeff Bezos’s Amazon shareholder letters** — one-way versus two-way doors, making reversible decisions with incomplete information, and “disagree and commit.” The [2016 letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders) is the most compact treatment.
- **The strategy literature on real options** — the deeper economic logic behind staged commitments, flexibility, and investing to preserve the right to decide later. McKinsey’s [“The Real Power of Real Options”](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-real-power-of-real-options) is a useful business-oriented introduction.

The most useful way to read these is not as competing schools. Each solves a different part of the same problem. Kahneman and Tversky explain why unaided judgment goes wrong. Tetlock shows how explicit probabilistic reasoning can improve it. Duke makes the distinction between decision and outcome intuitive. Klein improves the search for failure modes. Dalio addresses whose judgment should influence the group. Amazon addresses speed and decision rights. Real-options thinking addresses how much to commit while uncertainty remains.

Put together, they form a practical answer to a problem every serious organization faces: how to act decisively when the future is uncertain, without pretending that it is known.
